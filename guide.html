Weight management, explained

What medically supervised weight management actually involves

Telehealth GLP-1 programs went from niche to everywhere in about three years. Here is a plain look at how they work, what the process asks of you, and the questions worth asking before you hand over a card.

Team7 Health editorial · Updated September 2026 · 6 minute read

The short version

  • These are prescription programs. A licensed clinician reviews your history and decides whether medication is appropriate. Nobody is guaranteed a prescription.
  • Most low-cost programs supply compounded medication, which is not the same thing as the branded product and is not FDA-approved as a finished drug.
  • The intake is short. The commitment is not. Most programs bill monthly or annually.
  • Eligibility usually depends on BMI and medical history, and some conditions rule you out entirely.

Why appetite is the part people get wrong

The standard story about weight is a story about willpower. Eat less, move more, and the rest follows. It is a tidy story and it holds up badly, because appetite is not a character trait. It is a signalling system, and it is a stubborn one.

GLP-1 is a hormone your gut releases after you eat. Among other jobs, it tells your brain that food has arrived and that it can stop asking for more. GLP-1 medications work on that same receptor. They do not add discipline. They change how loud the signal is.

That distinction matters, because it reframes what the medication is for. It is not a shortcut around effort. It is a way of making the effort land on a system that is actually listening.

The medication is not the program. The supervision is the program.

What the process looks like

Every telehealth provider structures this slightly differently, but the shape is consistent.

1

A health questionnaire

Usually a few minutes. Height and weight, medical history, current medications, anything that might interact or disqualify you.

2

Clinician review

A licensed provider reads your intake. Some programs do this by video, some asynchronously. They decide whether treatment is appropriate, and they may decide it is not.

3

A plan, if prescribed

Dosing usually starts low and increases over the first weeks. This is deliberate. Starting high is how people end up with side effects they were not warned about.

4

Delivery and check-ins

Medication ships from a licensed pharmacy. Ongoing contact with the provider is where dose changes and side effects get handled.

Compounded and branded are not the same thing

This is the part most articles skip, and it is the part that explains the price.

Branded GLP-1 medications are manufactured by large pharmaceutical companies and approved by the FDA as finished products. They typically cost well over a thousand dollars a month without insurance.

Compounded medication is prepared by a licensed compounding pharmacy against an individual prescription. It uses the same active ingredient, but the finished preparation is not individually reviewed by the FDA for safety, effectiveness or quality. That is the trade being made when a program costs under two hundred dollars a month.

Branded Compounded
FDA review of the finished product Yes No
Prepared by Pharmaceutical manufacturer Licensed compounding pharmacy
Typical monthly cost without insurance Over $1,000 Commonly $89 to $400
Regulatory outlook Stable Under active review

Neither choice is automatically wrong. But you should know which one you are buying, and any program worth using will tell you plainly on its own site.

Who these programs suit, and who they do not

Eligibility is not a formality. Most programs screen on BMI, commonly around 27 and above, and medical history rules some people out regardless of what they weigh.

You are generally not eligible if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, if you have certain thyroid or endocrine conditions, or if you have a history of specific cancers. There are others. This is precisely what the intake is for, and it is why a program that promises a prescription before reviewing you is a program to walk away from.

Beyond eligibility, there is fit. These programs suit people who want structure and medical oversight rather than another plan to follow alone. They suit people who have tried the conventional route and found it did not hold. They suit less well anyone hoping for something that requires nothing of them, because dosing schedules, side effect management and regular check-ins all ask for attention.

Six things worth checking before you sign up

  • Compounded or branded. If the site does not say clearly, that is your answer about how much else it is not saying.
  • What the monthly price actually covers. Some providers price by dose, so month one and month four are different numbers.
  • The billing term. Monthly, three months and annual plans are all common, and the headline price is often the annual one.
  • Cancellation terms. Look for the notice period. Several programs require a few days' notice before the billing date.
  • Refund policy. Prescription medication generally cannot be returned once dispensed, so refunds are usually narrow.
  • Who the clinician is. Licensed in your state, and reachable after the first prescription, not only before it.

None of this is a reason to avoid these programs. It is a reason to read before you buy, which is true of anything that bills monthly.
